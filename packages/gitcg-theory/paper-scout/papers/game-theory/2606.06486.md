## Summary

Introduces **Repeated Policy Regret (RP-Regret)**, a new game-theoretic metric for repeated games where opponents adapt based on observed play history. Standard "external regret" from online learning fails when opponents can respond counterfactually — RP-Regret captures this by comparing realized utility against the best-in-hindsight strategy, accounting for opponents' adaptive responses.

Three algorithms proposed for minimizing RP-Regret despite its non-convex nature: (1) optimization oracle-based, (2) convex linearized surrogate, (3) direct minimization under slow opponent adaptation. When all players minimize RP-Regret, certain subgame perfect equilibria can be learned. Experiments on Stag-Hunt show convergence to cooperative, high-utility solutions.

## Key Contributions

- **RP-Regret**: Novel regret metric for repeated games with adaptive opponents — fills gap between external regret (too weak) and swap regret (too strong)
- **Provable algorithms**: Three approaches with theoretical guarantees for non-convex RP-Regret minimization
- **Equilibrium convergence**: Self-play with RP-Regret minimization converges to subgame perfect equilibria in certain settings
- **Cooperative outcomes**: RP-Regret promotes cooperative behavior (e.g., Stag-Hunt) better than standard regret minimization

## Connection to Our Work

- **GI TCG as repeated game**: Each GI TCG match is a repeated strategic interaction — rounds of card play with adaptive opponent responses. RP-Regret provides a theoretically grounded training objective for our agents.
- **Opponent adaptation**: Our GBDT ranker and LLM deck prior could be evaluated under RP-Regret — how well do they perform against adaptive opponents who learn from play history?
- **Training methodology**: The linearized surrogate approach (algorithm ii) could be applied to GI TCG policy optimization when exact RP-Regret computation is infeasible due to large state spaces.

## Critical Assessment

**Strengths**:
- Strong theoretical foundations with clear definitions and provable bounds
- Practical algorithms addressing non-convexity — the linearized surrogate is likely the most scalable
- Stag-Hunt experiments demonstrate real behavioral differences from standard regret

**Weaknesses**:
- Evaluated only on small matrix games (Stag-Hunt) — no demonstration on complex sequential games like card games
- RP-Regret computation requires knowing opponent strategies, which is challenging in partially observable settings like GI TCG
- No open-source code at time of publication
- Games tested are fully observable — extension to IIG unclear

**Reproducibility**: Low — no code release, limited experimental setup description. The theoretical contributions are clearly stated but practical implementation details are thin.
