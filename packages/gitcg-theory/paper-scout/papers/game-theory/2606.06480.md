## Summary

Proposes **DNQ (Deep Nash Q-Network)**, a solver-in-the-loop equilibrium supervision framework for training agents in partially observable n-player games. Uses multi-turn simultaneous bidding as a testbed (relevant to auctions, resource allocation, security games). The framework alternates between: trajectory collection, critic-based payoff estimation, Nash equilibrium computation, and policy imitation via KL divergence minimization.

Key innovation: a **pairwise payoff formulation** that dramatically reduces equilibrium-solving cost vs. exact N-player payoff tensor computation. A shared critic amortizes payoff learning across agents and states. The pairwise variant scales to larger player counts where exact methods become computationally intractable.

## Key Contributions

- **Solver-in-the-loop architecture**: Novel training paradigm where an external equilibrium solver provides supervision targets for neural policies
- **Scalable pairwise formulation**: Reduces equilibrium computation from exponential to quadratic in player count — enables N-player settings
- **Shared critic**: Amortizes payoff estimation across agents and states for sample efficiency
- **Empirical scalability analysis**: Clear demonstration of exact vs. pairwise trade-off in terms of training cost, policy entropy, and resource usage

## Connection to Our Work

- **Multi-agent GI TCG**: If we extend GI TCG AI to handle team play or 2v2 formats, DNQ's pairwise equilibrium formulation scales naturally
- **Solver-in-the-loop training**: GI TCG subgames (e.g., endgame with known cards) could be solved exactly and used as supervision targets, similar to DNQ's approach
- **Partially observable Nash**: GI TCG's hidden opponent hand/dice creates partial observability — DNQ's framework handles this natively
- **Deck selection as bidding game**: The deck-building/selection phase could be modeled as a simultaneous-move game where DNQ-style equilibrium training applies

## Critical Assessment

**Strengths**:
- Clean architecture with clear separation of concerns (trajectory, critic, solver, policy)
- Pairwise formulation is genuinely practical — makes N-player settings feasible
- Well-designed experiments comparing exact vs. pairwise, showing clear scaling trade-offs

**Weaknesses**:
- Bidding domain is simpler than card games — sequential actions, complex state transitions not addressed
- No evaluation against human players or existing bidding strategies
- Payoff estimation accuracy not deeply analyzed — errors in critic propagate to poor equilibrium targets
- No open-source code
- Equilibrium computation overhead may be prohibitive for real-time play (though not needed at inference)

**Reproducibility**: Low — no code, but architecture is clearly described. Implementing the solver-in-the-loop pipeline would be non-trivial.
